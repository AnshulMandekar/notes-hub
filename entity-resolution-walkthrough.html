Entity Resolution Walkthrough
End-to-end simulation · the project's own code · Vermont + Punjab slice

Follow one record through the whole matching pipeline

We ran the project's real training command, with the final submission settings, on every business in Vermont and Punjab from the training data. It finished in 51 seconds. Then we traced six records through every step. Pick one below: each step shows the idea in plain words on the left and what actually happened to that record on the right.

Follow

    Step 0

    The task

    Entity resolution means deciding which records describe the same real-world thing. Here the things are businesses.

    • Source 1 is a clean list with each business once.
    • Sources 2 and 3 are messy copies: typos, missing parts, other scripts, reordered addresses.
    • The ground truth file lists, for each Source 1 business, which messy records belong to it. The model learns from these answers.
    • A decoy is a messy record that belongs to no business, often a near-copy of a real one.
    • A singleton is a business with no messy records at all. The right answer for it is an empty list.

    Step 1

    Clean the text

    normalize.py · indic.py · translit.py

    Normalisation rewrites every spelling variant into one standard form, so equal things look equal. Both sides go through the same rules. Real examples from this slice:

    Transliteration turns Indian scripts into Latin letters by sound: ਲਕਸ਼ਮੀ becomes lakasami. The project wrote its own converter for 9 scripts.

    The learned dictionary fixes words that are English written in Punjabi letters. Sound alone gives kansatrakasan, not “construction”, so the pipeline learned these pairs from the training answers.

    A phonetic skeleton keeps a word's consonant sounds and drops the vowels, so different spellings of one sound agree: lakasami and lakshmi both become lksm.

    Step 2

    Drop noise words

    noise.py

    Some words are added by whatever produced the messy sources and almost never appear in Source 1. To a similarity measure they look rare and important, which makes true matches look different. The pipeline finds them from the data, with no labels:

    ratio = (share of Source 2/3 records with the word) ÷ (share of Source 1 records with it)

    A word with a ratio of 20 or more that appears often enough is removed from addresses. Name words are only reported, not removed: on the training data words like “midtown” turned out to mark decoys, so they are evidence worth keeping.

    Step 3

    Turn each record into tokens

    blocking.py · record_tokens

    A token is a small searchable key. Each record becomes a bag of tokens, and two records are similar when they share tokens. Several kinds of token catch different kinds of noise:

    • Words and skeletons survive spelling changes.
    • Word pairs keep records made of common words searchable: “best care” is rarer than “best” or “care” alone.
    • Skip pairs in addresses survive an inserted word: “84 [Old] Lake” still gives 84_lake.
    • State + word tokens make a chain name prefer the branch in the same state.

    Step 4

    Weigh tokens by rarity (IDF)

    Sharing a rare token means much more than sharing a common one. Two records that both say “Hicks” are probably related; two that both say “Road” tell you nothing. IDF (inverse document frequency) turns rarity into a weight:

    idf = ln( (N + 1) ÷ (df + 1) ) + 1

    N is the number of records in the country and df is how many of them contain the token. A token in 1 record gets a high weight; a token in thousands gets a low one.

    Tokens in more than Source 1 records (0.2% at full scale) are not indexed at all, because they are too common to narrow the search. Tokens that no Source 1 record has cannot lead anywhere, so they are ignored too.

    This record's tokens with weights
    Step 5

    Find candidates (blocking)

    blocking.py · sparse_dot_topn

    Comparing every record with every business is too slow, so blocking makes a short list of likely businesses for each record.

    Each record becomes a vector: one number per token, equal to its IDF weight. Cosine similarity measures how much two vectors point the same way, from 0 (nothing shared) to 1 (identical):

    cosine = (sum of weight² over shared tokens) ÷ (length of record vector × length of business vector)

    The pipeline runs five searches inside each country and keeps the top K of each:

    • name and address together, top 10
    • name only, top 5 (finds records with a wrong or missing address)
    • address only, top 5 (finds renamed businesses)
    • 3-letter pieces of the name, top 1 (finds badly misspelt names)
    • borrowed from look-alike records with the same name skeleton and house number, top 3

    The union of these lists is the candidate set. Recall is the share of true pairs that made it in. A missed pair can never be matched later.

    Step 6

    Measure each pair (features)

    features.py

    A feature is one number that describes a pair. The pipeline computes 80 of them for every candidate pair. The main ideas:

    • Character match (edit distance): how many letters must change to turn one name into the other. “Autodtiie” vs “Automotive” scores lower than “Autmotive”.
    • Word-set match ignores word order and repeats: “Holdings-Group Optify” equals “Optify Holdings Group”.
    • Partial match finds the best matching piece, so a short name inside a longer one still scores high.
    • Jaro-Winkler gives extra credit when the start of the names agrees, where typos are rarer.
    • Word alignment pairs up the words and counts the ones left over, plus how rare the rarest leftover is. A leftover “Services” is cheap; a leftover “Dental” is a warning.
    • House numbers: equal, or how far apart. 1127 vs 1126 is next door; 1893 vs nothing is just missing.
    • Context: the search rank, and how far this candidate is ahead of the next one.

    No feature says which country a record is from, so the model treats France (never seen in training) like any other country.

    Step 7

    Score every pair (stage 1)

    model.py · LightGBM

    LightGBM builds many small decision trees. Each tree asks a few yes/no questions about the features and nudges a score up or down. The next tree corrects what is still wrong. The total score becomes a probability between 0 and 1. (The separate LightGBM simulator shows this tree by tree.)

    Training data split by business, so held-out businesses are never seen during training in any form:

    Stage 1 trained on 381,184 candidate pairs (24,871 true) of the stage-1 businesses and stopped early after 158 trees when the early-stop group stopped improving.

    Step 8

    One record, one business

    In the data, a messy record belongs to at most one business. So each record keeps only its highest-scoring candidate, and every other candidate is dropped. This removes many possible false matches for free, which matters because the metric punishes wrong matches twice as hard as missed ones.

    This record's winner
    Step 9

    Double-check with context (stage 2)

    stage2.py

    Stage 1 judged each pair alone. Stage 2 is a second LightGBM model that sees the neighbourhood of each winner:

    • Margin: how far the winner beat the runner-up.
    • Competition: how many other records also picked this business, and how confident they are.
    • Siblings: those other records are usually the business's other copies. If this record looks like a confident sibling, it is probably true too.
    • Sibling agreement: where this record differs from the business (another house number, an extra word), do the siblings side with the record or with the business? Random noise hits one record; a decoy's details repeat only inside its own group.

    Stage 2 trained on a separate 40% of businesses and uses 105 features: the 80 pair features plus 25 context features. Its main clues:

    Step 10

    Decide

    run.py threshold

    A threshold turns the probability into a yes or no. A match is accepted when the stage-2 probability is at least 0.85.

    On held-out training data the best threshold was lower (0.61), but the test set had about one extra decoy per business. A stricter cut-off gave up a few true matches to avoid many false ones, and leaderboard uploads confirmed it (0.85 beat 0.69).

    The house-number guard adds a stricter bar when the record's house number is 1 to 10 away from the business's: 0.999 in the US and 0.98 elsewhere. The test set had 2.4 to 2.6 times more of these next-door records than the training data. On training-like data, like this slice, the guard costs a little, so it is a bet on the test set.

    This record's decision
    Step 11

    Write the answer and score it

    run.py · evaluate.py

    Accepted matches are grouped by business into matching_results.tsv: one line per Source 1 business, a tab, then its matched ids separated by commas. The shortlists from step 5 go into candidate_pairs.tsv.

    Each business is scored on its own:

    • Precision (P): of the records we matched to it, the share that are right.
    • Recall (R): of its true records, the share we found.
    F0.5 = 1.25 × P × R ÷ (0.25 × P + R)

    F0.5 weighs precision twice as much as recall. A singleton scores 1 for an empty answer and 0 for any match. The final score is the plain average over all businesses (macro average), so a small business counts as much as a big one.
